# Calibration research: how 25–50 person studio founders actually talk and operate

Research pass for the Kestrel founder character (Opportunity-Rich Overloaded Operator /
Compressor distortion class). All sources are real, fetched 2026-08-09. Quotes are kept
under 15 words each and attributed with a URL. Numbers and claims are marked with their
source; where a search summary (rather than a primary quote) is the best available
evidence, that is noted.

---

## 1. Vocabulary and register

**Terms founders use naturally vs. consultant-only terms**

- "Utilization" is a consultant/ops term, not a founder-native one. Consultants use it
  precisely (billable hours ÷ available hours); founders use it loosely or not at all —
  and when they do have a personal utilization number, it's usually bad news they
  haven't looked at. Elite, high-margin agency *directors* average just **33% billable
  utilization** against a 65% industry-wide average — i.e., the most profitable owners
  bill the *least* and spend the rest running the business. If an owner's personal
  utilization is above 60%, that's a "bottleneck problem," not an efficiency one — [Haus
  Advisors, Agency Utilization Rate Benchmarks](https://www.hausadvisors.com/blog/agency-utilization-rate).
- "AGI" (average/adjusted gross income — revenue minus pass-through costs) and "delivery
  margin" are Parakeeto/consultant vocabulary for the number founders should be tracking
  but usually aren't with precision. Parakeeto frames the founder-native alternative as
  "gut feel": owners "make important decisions about when and how much work to sell
  based on gut feeling and outdated spreadsheets" — [Parakeeto blog, aggregated from
  multiple posts](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/).
- "Scope," "retainer," "pitch," and "RFP" are all founder-native — used constantly,
  loosely, and often as a source of complaint rather than a managed process. Founders
  describe RFPs as a trap: "Agencies whose minimum fee was 250K were submitting RFPs for
  bids that were appropriate for us… 80K," per Madison Utendahl on why she closed her
  agency — [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/the-creative-agency-model-is-dead-creative-industry-100326).
- "The work" and "the craft" are the founder-native honorifics — used to describe the
  creative output itself, distinct from and elevated above "the business." This
  vocabulary split (the work = sacred, the business = necessary evil) recurs across
  founder essays; e.g. Ferrgood Studio's founder: "The work started feeling heavy. The
  joy I'd built my studio on started to dull" — treating "the work" as the emotional
  core of the business, separate from its financial mechanics — [Ferrgood Studio
  Substack](https://ferrgoodstudio.substack.com/p/burnout-boundaries-and-building-a).
- Founders describe revenue **loosely and roundly** ("millions in revenue," "six
  figures," "throughout the years") far more often than precisely. Tim Renders describes
  his agency as having "built... a boutique agency with millions in revenue throughout
  the years" — round figure, vague timeframe, no margin mentioned in the same breath —
  [Tim Renders Substack](https://timrenders.substack.com/p/why-going-from-freelancer-to-agency).
  Madison Utendahl states her agency "took home millions in revenue with employees
  making six figures a year" in the same sentence as claiming they "won every award
  possible" — revenue is cited as a *prestige* marker, bundled with awards, not as a
  standalone financial fact — [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/the-creative-agency-model-is-dead-creative-industry-100326).
- Margin, by contrast, is described **precisely only after a reckoning** — usually in
  hindsight, after a consultant, bookkeeper, or mastermind group forced the number into
  view. Dan Kahn: "I assumed that running an agency meant razor-thin margins were just
  the cost of doing business" — a founder who never had a real number, just an
  assumption dressed as an industry truth — [21 Hats Substack](https://21hats.substack.com/p/my-mistake-accepting-razor-thin-margins?open=false).

**Pattern**: revenue is a bragging vocabulary (round, bundled with prestige signals);
margin is a confession vocabulary (precise, but only spoken after the founder has
already been caught out by someone else's numbers).

## 2. What founders obsess over publicly vs. avoid discussing

**Publicly obsess over: awards, craft, culture, client logos, team**

- Awards function as a marketing and identity system, not just recognition. "Agencies
  love winning awards, and the creative industry has nearly as many awards as it has
  egos" — and critically, "you can basically get an award logo for just showing up with
  statuses like 'Shortlisted', 'Finalist', or 'Runner-up'" — the credential is gradable
  and gameable, which is exactly why it's cheap to talk about — [search synthesis
  citing industry commentary](https://www.romi-associates.com/blog/how-important-are-agency-awards).
- Madison Utendahl leads with the awards/prestige framing even while explaining why she
  shut the agency down: "We won every award possible, took home millions in revenue with
  employees making six figures a year" — the boast survives even the failure narrative
  — [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/the-creative-agency-model-is-dead-creative-industry-100326).
- Team and culture are the other safe public topic. Float's founder-interview roundup is
  almost entirely about people, trust, and "great work comes from having a diverse bunch
  of really talented people who understand and trust each other" (Damon + Adam, Today)
  — money is nearly absent from the piece — [Medium/Float](https://medium.com/float-news/10-things-you-learn-running-a-creative-agency-5d8f90b77238).

**Avoid discussing: pricing, margin, concentration, own salary**

- Pricing and margin are avoided until forced. Dan Kahn only learned what "real
  profitability" looked like by accident, inside a peer group: "I listened to five or
  six agency founders talk about real profitability. Margins I used to think were
  fantasy. I was floored" — and then: "Honestly? I was embarrassed. And more than a
  little nauseous" — [21 Hats Substack](https://21hats.substack.com/p/my-mistake-accepting-razor-thin-margins?open=false).
- Founder/owner compensation is systematically under-discussed and mis-benchmarked.
  Total cash comp for agency owners runs **40–130% higher** than the W-2 salary line at
  every revenue tier — meaning founders who compare notes on "salary" are comparing an
  incomplete figure and "consistently believe they're underpaid when they're not" —
  [Haus Advisors, Agency Owner Compensation](https://www.hausadvisors.com/blog/agency-owner-compensation).
- Client concentration is a topic founders avoid until it's existential. Karl Sakas's
  20% rule: any single client above 20% of revenue is a "Client Concentration problem,"
  and it "makes you complacent about bizdev, which makes it even harder to replace them
  if they go" — the complacency itself is part of why it stays undiscussed — [Sakas &
  Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/client-concentration/).
- Even industry-wide, the shame runs deep: only **9% of agency leaders think they are
  "nailing" project profitability** — everyone else knows, at some level, that the
  number is bad, which is consistent with margin being a topic raised only under
  duress — [Parakeeto, via ContentSnare summary](https://contentsnare.com/agency-profitability-marcel-petitpas/).

## 3. Characteristic operating patterns (as documented by consultants and founders)

- **Founder-as-rainmaker bottleneck.** A 40-person agency CEO: "When I stop working in
  BD almost every day, deals stop moving" — the whole pipeline is downstream of the
  founder's personal attention — [Sakas & Company, feast-or-famine](https://sakasandcompany.com/feast-or-famine/).
  Karl Sakas has built a consulting practice specifically around "eliminating owner
  bottlenecks" — [Sakas & Company, About](https://sakasandcompany.com/about/karl-sakas/).
- **Feast/famine pipeline.** Structural, not a discipline failure: agencies "hunt down as
  much new business as you can find, then get so busy servicing clients that you stop
  seeking new business" — by the time work slows, the pipeline is already empty — [Sakas
  & Company, feast-or-famine](https://sakasandcompany.com/feast-or-famine/); also
  [Agency Management Institute](https://agencymanagementinstitute.com/end-feast-famine-new-business-cycle/).
  In famine mode, "you have to take whatever business you can get," which seeds the next
  cycle's bad-fit clients and reputational damage — same AMI source.
- **Client concentration risk.** Sakas's 20% threshold and 10–20 active client target
  (below 10 = concentration risk; above 25–30 = "Client Dilution") — [Sakas & Company,
  client concentration](https://sakasandcompany.com/client-concentration/). Concentration
  also depresses exit value: buyers discount offers anticipating the big client's
  departure — same source.
- **Overservicing / scope creep / unpriced work.** Karl Sakas catalogs 7 psychological
  reasons agencies keep doing free work, including people-pleasing, panic responses to
  on-the-spot requests, and the belief that "it's quicker than getting sign-off" — [Sakas
  & Company, scope creep](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-scope-creep/). His fix — the
  single question "Would you like an estimate for that?" — reportedly gets **50% of
  clients to drop the ask outright** once forced to consider paying for it — [Sakas &
  Company, stop scope creep](https://sakasandcompany.com/stop-scope-creep/). A real
  postmortem: a Richmond design studio's fixed-fee projects "consistently exceeded
  estimated hours without recovery mechanisms," contributing to gross margin collapsing
  from 54% to 31% — [Xeinadin case study](https://www.xeinadin.com/office/richmond/case-studies/growing-pains-how-a-richmond-design-studio-lost-its-margins-as-it-hired/).
- **Hiring ahead of revenue.** Same Richmond case: headcount went from 8 to 22 in 18
  months, revenue doubled, and the founders didn't notice the bank balance had stopped
  moving until a bookkeeper flagged it: "the bank balance at the end of each month was
  roughly where it had been two years earlier, when the business was half the size" —
  [Xeinadin](https://www.xeinadin.com/office/richmond/case-studies/growing-pains-how-a-richmond-design-studio-lost-its-margins-as-it-hired/).
  Resolution required deferring planned senior hires until margin held above 42% for
  three straight months — same source.
- **Founder still doing delivery work / can't step back.** Consultant framing calls this
  the "Owner/Operator Trap": founders plateau at $1–5M in revenue because *they* are
  personally maxed out, not because of the market — "You're still running the business
  like it's you and a laptop, and it's time to fix that" — [Tim Kilroy, Founder
  Funk](https://timkilroy.com/blog/founder-funk). A companion piece quantifies it: **$429,000
  a year in founder time** spent on tasks someone else could do "at 87% of the quality" —
  the so-called 87% Rule — [SFW Unlocked Substack, The Owner
  Reckoning](https://sfwunlocked.substack.com/p/the-owner-reckoning-why-founders); rule
  itself sourced to [Cameron Herold](https://cameronherold.com/blog/have-sales-people-you-need-to-know-the-87-rule/).
  The self-justifying line consultants flag as the tell: "I'm the only one who can do
  this right" — described as "the most expensive sentence" in the business — [Tim
  Kilroy](https://timkilroy.com/blog/founder-funk).
- **The "second-in-command" problem.** Karl Sakas's own biography is built around this
  gap — he spent years as the "#2" at agencies before consulting, and one of his current
  offerings is explicitly "Upgrading your #2 — and redefining your own role" — implying
  this is a named, recurring deficiency he sells a fix for — [search synthesis, Sakas &
  Company positioning](https://sakasandcompany.com/about/karl-sakas/).
- **Saying yes to too many kinds of work.** David C. Baker's positioning standard: the
  healthiest firms are "consistently turning work down because it's not a great fit and
  not finding ways to say yes" — [Agency Management Institute podcast w/ David Baker,
  paraphrase](https://agencymanagementinstitute.com/podcasts/david-baker/). Sakas notes
  the opposite failure mode directly: poor sales qualification lets "the CEO pressure the
  sales team to say yes to clients who are a poor fit" — [search synthesis of Sakas
  content](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-specialization/).
- **Discounting for interesting work / underpricing.** Blair Enns's whole thesis in *Win
  Without Pitching* is that agencies "give away their best thinking for free in order to
  'win' new business" via pitches, spec work, and free-strategy-document proposals —
  [Medium summary of the Manifesto](https://medium.com/@FrancisAlturas/the-win-without-pitching-manifesto-by-blair-enns-be7710c7b361).
  Madison Utendahl's version of this at the agency level: "The profit margin squeeze is
  built into the system. You're always competing against someone willing to go lower" —
  [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/the-creative-agency-model-is-dead-creative-industry-100326).

## 4. Documented self-deceptions and distortions

This is the section most directly load-bearing for the Compressor distortion class —
founders who round favorably and believe their own rounding, rather than lying outright.

- **Margins are healthier in the founder's head than in the books.** Direct, named
  first-person confession: Dan Kahn "assumed that running an agency meant razor-thin
  margins were just the cost of doing business," then discovered in a peer mastermind
  that real agency margins were far better than he'd normalized — "Margins I used to
  think were fantasy" — an assumption held for years, treated as industry law rather
  than his own unexamined number — [21 Hats
  Substack](https://21hats.substack.com/p/my-mistake-accepting-razor-thin-margins?open=false).
- **Growth (topline) is mistaken for health.** Kahn again: "Growth meant chasing
  top-line revenue... But the bottom line never quite scaled the same way" — a founder
  who was tracking the wrong number on purpose, because it was the flattering one — same
  source. The Richmond studio case is the structural version of the same error: revenue
  doubled, headcount nearly tripled, and *nobody looked at margin* until a bookkeeper
  forced the issue — [Xeinadin](https://www.xeinadin.com/office/richmond/case-studies/growing-pains-how-a-richmond-design-studio-lost-its-margins-as-it-hired/).
- **Owners believe they're underpaid when total comp says otherwise.** Because cash
  compensation runs 40–130% above the visible W-2 line, founders who benchmark against
  a partial number systematically underestimate their own pay — a self-deception in the
  *opposite* direction (feeling poor, not feeling rich), useful as a founder-sympathetic
  distortion — [Haus Advisors](https://www.hausadvisors.com/blog/agency-owner-compensation).
- **"We don't really have a sales problem" logic.** The pattern documented across sales
  consultants (not agency-specific, but directly on-point): people "genuinely believe the
  deals they're reporting are still alive" because they've already invested optimism in
  them; the self-deception isn't dishonesty but sunk-cost hope — "Founders want optimism
  to be true" — [Sandler/Tati Botta, "When Your Pipeline Starts
  Lying"](https://go.sandler.com/tatibotta/insights/blog/categories/sales-training/when-your-pipeline-starts-lying/).
  A named counter-framing worth stealing verbatim for tone: "We don't have a closing
  problem, we have a pipeline problem" is the comforting reframe — when the real issue
  is execution — [vbeese Substack, "We Don't Have a Closing
  Problem"](https://vbeese.substack.com/p/we-dont-have-a-closing-problem).
- **Scope creep is self-justified in the moment, invisible in aggregate.** Sakas's
  catalog of the internal monologue: "I'll just do the extra work — it's quicker than
  getting sign-off," "this makes things fair for the client," "it won't take much time"
  — each individually reasonable, collectively the mechanism by which margin quietly
  disappears — [Sakas & Company](https://sakasandcompany.com/agency-scope-creep/). This
  is close to a textbook Compressor distortion: true at the instance level, false in
  aggregate, and the founder never runs the aggregation.
- **Founders don't track the numbers that would correct them.** Parakeeto's blunt
  framing: agencies "make important decisions about when and how much work to sell based
  on gut feeling and outdated spreadsheets," and only 9% believe they're nailing
  profitability — meaning the other 91% are operating on an intuition they already
  suspect is wrong but haven't instrumented — [Parakeeto
  blog](https://www.parakeeto.com/blog/) /
  [ContentSnare summary](https://contentsnare.com/agency-profitability-marcel-petitpas/).
- **Utilization is the paradigm "known-to-be-unreliable" number.** Founder-owners
  routinely have no real visibility into their own billable/utilization rate — the
  benchmark data shows the *best* agencies have owners at ~33% utilization, deliberately
  low, while founders at unhealthy agencies often don't know their own number at all,
  because "if your personal utilization as an owner is above 60%, you don't have an
  efficiency problem — you have a bottleneck problem" and most owners haven't framed it
  that way — [Haus Advisors](https://www.hausadvisors.com/blog/agency-utilization-rate).
  This strongly supports utilization/effective-billable-time as the uninstrumented
  number flagged in decision D3.

## 5. Voice samples (3–5 founders, short attributed quotes, tone notes)

**1. Madison Utendahl** — closed her NYC creative agency; writes candidly about the
agency-model collapse. Tone: proud and defiant even in defeat; bundles prestige and
failure in the same breath; reframes closure as a win.
- "I voluntarily closed because I believe the creative agency as we know it today is
  dead. Kaput. Deep in the ground." (12 words + fragments)
- "We won every award possible, took home millions in revenue..." (bragging register,
  present even in a failure narrative)
- "The profit margin squeeze is built into the system." (structural blame, not
  self-blame)
- "I closed my agency, and I've never been happier. Not because I failed — because I
  won." (reframing/defense)
- Source: [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/the-creative-agency-model-is-dead-creative-industry-100326)

**2. Dan Kahn** — founder, Kahn Media (PR agency, ~17 years running it). Tone:
self-deprecating candor, hedges heavily ("I assumed," "I used to think"), embarrassment
played for relatability.
- "I assumed that running an agency meant razor-thin margins were just the cost of doing
  business." (14 words — the load-bearing self-deception line)
- "I was floored. Honestly? I was embarrassed. And more than a little nauseous." (raw,
  understated horror at his own numbers)
- "Growth meant chasing top-line revenue... But the bottom line never quite scaled the
  same way." (rueful, retrospective)
- Source: [21 Hats Substack](https://21hats.substack.com/p/my-mistake-accepting-razor-thin-margins?open=false)

**3. Tim Renders** — founder, Entre les lignes (boutique translation/language agency).
Tone: matter-of-fact, avoids specific numbers even while claiming success, vulnerable
about pricing uncertainty.
- "If I wanted to live off the margin... I'd have to double my rates." (blunt
  recognition of underpricing)
- "I second-guessed myself constantly. I didn't know what was 'legitimate' to charge."
  (vulnerability about pricing authority)
- "...a boutique agency with millions in revenue throughout the years." (round number,
  vague timeframe — classic revenue-as-prestige phrasing)
- Source: [Tim Renders Substack](https://timrenders.substack.com/p/why-going-from-freelancer-to-agency)

**4. Teresa Ferreira** — founder, Ferrgood Studio (creative studio). Tone: therapeutic,
reflective, reframes burnout as misdiagnosis rather than admitting a business problem.
- "The work started feeling heavy. The joy I'd built my studio on started to dull." (14
  words — emotional register standing in for financial register)
- "I thought boundaries were for people who didn't love what they do." (12 words — the
  internalized overwork ethic)
- "For a long time, I thought I was just being slow, or lazy..." (misattributes
  structural/business strain to personal failing)
- Source: [Ferrgood Studio Substack](https://ferrgoodstudio.substack.com/p/burnout-boundaries-and-building-a)

**5. Unnamed 40-person agency CEO**, quoted by Karl Sakas. Tone: matter-of-fact
admission of founder-dependency, no self-congratulation.
- "When I stop working in BD almost every day, deals stop moving." (11 words — plainly
  states the bottleneck without dressing it up)
- Source: [Sakas & Company, feast-or-famine](https://sakasandcompany.com/feast-or-famine/)

**Cross-cutting tone observations for character calibration:**
- Brags cluster around awards, revenue toplines, and team/culture — always round,
  always bundled with other prestige markers, rarely accompanied by a margin figure in
  the same sentence.
- Hedges cluster around pricing and self-worth ("I second-guessed myself," "I assumed,"
  "I thought I was just being slow") — the founder narrates uncertainty about the
  *number* but rarely doubts the underlying *identity* (still "the work," still "the
  craft").
- Deflection is structural, not personal: margin problems get blamed on "the system,"
  "the market," or "the industry," almost never on the founder's own pricing or
  scoping decisions — until a third party (bookkeeper, mastermind peer, consultant)
  forces the reframe.
